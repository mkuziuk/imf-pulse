## Signal 01 — The robust object is a point, not a component

**What changed.** The paper defines a local polynomial approach (LPA) M-estimator by fitting a polynomial inside a kernel-weighted neighborhood of x0. Its contrast must have a bounded Lipschitz score; the Huber contrast is the main example. A Lepski comparison rule then selects among a dyadic family of bandwidths by checking when finer-scale point estimates remain within calibrated thresholds.

**Why it matters.** This supplies a clean external definition of local robustness: extreme residuals have capped influence while the target remains the regression value f(x0). That is narrower than an intrinsic robust multiscale filtering recursion, whose target changes after every residual update. The estimator can therefore inform a single local fit, but it cannot be inserted into a recursion with its guarantees intact.

**Evidence.** [Primary paper, p. 3, 15](/sources#src-external-arxiv-1105-1646v1).

**Confidence.** High for the estimator and bandwidth rule because both are explicit in the criterion and selection equations.

**Assumptions and limitations.** Design points are independent and uniform, errors are independent, and error densities are symmetric and nonincreasing on the positive half-line.; The contrast has a bounded 1-Lipschitz derivative and the kernel is positive, compactly supported, and normalized.; Robustness here means bounded-score resistance to extreme noise values around the pointwise target f(x0). Limitations: The calibration threshold contains constants tied to the noise-density curvature condition.; The rule addresses one point and one bandwidth family, not sequential residualization.

## Signal 02 — The rate is proved inside a narrow boundary

**What changed.** A new exponential deviation inequality supports two asymptotic risk results. With smoothness known, the fixed-bandwidth estimator reaches the pointwise rate n^{-beta/(2 beta+d)} over an isotropic Hölder class. With smoothness unknown, the Lepski-selected estimator attains the paper's adaptive normalization, including the usual logarithmic price away from the maximum regularity.

**Why it matters.** The result answers a local calibration question under unknown heavy-tailed noise without estimating the full noise density. It does not answer the accepted pulse's recursive question: how a clean same-contrast reference differs from an expected-noisy-loss target across stages, or whether bootstrap and Jacobian scales remain calibrated after residuals become dependent and nonstationary.

**Evidence.** [Primary paper, p. 10, 12, 15](/sources#src-external-arxiv-1105-1646v1).

**Confidence.** High for the theorem statements and their stated assumptions; no empirical performance claim is made.

**Assumptions and limitations.** The target is pointwise regression f(x0), risk is asymptotic maximal pointwise risk, and f lies in the stated isotropic Hölder class.; A positive lower bound on the density-curvature constant is available for the adaptive threshold.; The paper's heavy-tail model is symmetric-noise regression, not adversarial or epsilon contamination. Limitations: There is no numerical experiment, contamination stress test, or recursive-filter analysis.; The Huber threshold gamma is not chosen optimally; the paper names data-driven gamma selection as an open problem.; No result is transferred from this pointwise estimator to adaptive or robust recursion.

## Why this matters

The useful advance is a boundary, not a turnkey algorithm for intrinsic mode functions. The paper shows how bounded-score local fitting, a precise pointwise target, and data-driven bandwidth selection can coexist with asymptotic guarantees under symmetric heavy-tailed noise. For intrinsic robust multiscale filtering, the next test is therefore concrete: first verify the paper's single-stage target and calibration under the project's window and contamination choices, then measure how residualization changes the effective noise law before claiming that any pointwise rate survives across stages.
